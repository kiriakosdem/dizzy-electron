How does sound travel?

A vibration becomes a pressure wave

The membrane pushes nearby particles. They collide with their neighbors, passing on momentum and energy. A crowded region of higher pressure, a compression, travels through the gas. As the membrane moves back, a less crowded region, a rarefaction, follows.

The wave travels; the air does not travel with it

Air particles already move randomly even in silence. Sound adds a coordinated back-and-forth motion along the direction of travel: a longitudinal wave. Follow one particle to distinguish its motion from the moving density pattern.

Reading the picture

The bands and graph show how pressure differs from the current average across the chamber. Orange marks higher pressure and blue marks lower pressure. The average sits at the center of the graph, with the same fixed scale above and below it. The estimate uses random particle motion and momentum transferred in collisions. Particle color shows speed.

Try it

Select Single pulse, reset, then press Emit single pulse. Follow the compression along the chamber. Pause or slow playback to inspect collisions. Increase amplitude for a stronger disturbance, or frequency for more frequent compressions.

Where does the energy go?

Particles that cross the open end leave with their energy. New particles enter from a thermal reservoir. The inflow slowly adjusts using the average particle count to restore the starting population over time. A compression can still send more particles out than are entering. Collisions with the top, bottom and membrane are elastic in the frame of the surface.

About this model

The model uses identical disks in two dimensions. Particle collisions are elastic and create the wave. Distances and time (τ) use model units. Relative gas energy compares total kinetic energy with its value at reset.
