The Hydrogen Atom

Hydrogen orbitals

The wavefunction ψ gives the probability density |ψ|². A denser part of the cloud represents a greater chance of detecting the electron in a small volume there. These are stationary hydrogen states with a fixed nucleus, without spin or external fields. The complex-state cloud samples |RnℓYℓm|².

Complex states with m and −m have the same density. The real orbitals form an orthonormal basis made from the m = 0 state and normalized sine and cosine combinations of each ±m pair. Each combination has definite n and ℓ. For example, the familiar px and py orbitals each combine m = +1 and m = −1.

Surface and fuzzy cloud colors show the phase of ψ in a stationary snapshot. Orange represents phase 0 (+1) and light blue represents phase π (−1), with a continuous rainbow between them. Real orbitals have these two phases. For complex states with nonzero m, phase winds around z. A common overall phase leaves the probability density unchanged.

Choosing an orbital

Choose n from 1 to 6, ℓ from 0 to n − 1, and m from −ℓ to ℓ. The + and − buttons on the canvas change one number at a time. A disabled button marks a step outside these ranges. The Controls menus keep the other numbers wherever possible and adjust them when needed.

Scroll or swipe through the suggested real orbitals to see all 2ℓ + 1 choices. Select a numerical m in Controls to return to a complex eigenstate. For g and h orbitals, 0 denotes m = 0. The suffixes ks and kc denote the sin(kφ) and cos(kφ) combinations.

Reading the visualization

Point cloud. Each sphere marks one possible electron position sampled from |ψ|². Increasing point density adds samples and reveals finer detail in the same probability distribution. Sphere sizes are illustrative.

Detection highlights. An amber sphere marks one sampled position at a time, ten times per second. Each highlight represents a measurement on a separately prepared atom. The positions do not trace a trajectory or show how an electron evolves after a measurement.

Fuzzy cloud. Denser regions appear more opaque. Lower fuzziness sharpens dense regions. Higher fuzziness reveals a softer halo.

Phase-colored surface. The surface bounds regions above a common probability-density threshold. The enclosed probability setting gives the approximate probability of finding the electron inside those regions combined. Increasing it includes more of the faint outer regions. Nodal gaps are excluded, and outer shells can hide inner boundaries. The electron can still be found outside the surface.

Nodal surfaces. Translucent green marks surfaces where the probability density vanishes. The count includes radial shells, polar cones or planes, and the azimuthal planes of real orbitals. An isolated zero at the nucleus or along an axis contributes no surface. The display shows a finite portion of each plane or cone.

View controls. The z axis is the reference direction for m. Changing orbitals or display modes preserves the viewing angle and zoom, so higher-n states appear larger. Zoom in to inspect smaller states. Reset view restores the initial angle and scale.
